You Asked

Why did the Lions take a safety instead of the ball?

Tito Interpretation

Third and 11, Chicago on its own 9, tie game, 1:25 left in the half. Caleb Williams is sacked at the 2 and the ball comes loose into the Chicago end zone. A Bears player bats the loose ball in the end zone, it squirts back out to the 15, and Detroit recovers. Flag: illegal bat. And now Detroit holds a choice almost no fan knows exists: keep the recovery, first down at the Chicago 15, or enforce the foul where it happened, in the end zone, and take the safety. Detroit took the safety.

Detroit did not take two points instead of the ball. Detroit took two points and the ball. The safety broke the 10-10 tie, and after a safety the team scored upon has to put the ball in play with a free kick from its own 20. Chicago kicked, and Detroit took over at its own 29 with 1:13 left in the half. First down at the 15 is a chance at six points. The safety is two points in hand, the lead, and possession right back. Dan Campbell took the sure thing the book put on the table.

Keep the stakes straight: this was the second quarter of a tie game between two 8-2 teams playing for first place in the NFC North, not a stolen win. But it is the cleanest example you will see all season of an offense fouling in its own end zone, because the defense got to choose how it hurt.

Why Everyone Gets It Wrong

Fans think of a safety as something that happens to an offense: the quarterback is tackled in the end zone, or the ball rolls out the back. They miss that a safety can also be chosen. When the offense commits a foul behind its own goal line, the defense holds the election, and the recovery is only one side of the option. Fans also read a batted ball in a scramble as heads-up hustle. In the field of play a bat has narrow limits, but a loose ball that has touched the ground in either end zone cannot be batted or punched in any direction at all. The moment the ball was batted in Chicago's end zone, the foul, not the recovery, ran the play.

Rule Book Confirmed

Rule 12, Section 5, Article 1(b) makes it an illegal bat if any player bats or punches a loose ball that has touched the ground in any direction, if it is in either end zone. The penalty for illegal batting is a loss of 10 yards (Rule 12, Section 5, Article 2), and the game book shows exactly that: illegal bat, 10 yards, accepted. But the yardage is not the enforcement that mattered. Rule 11, Section 5, Article 1(a) provides that it is a safety when the offense commits a foul behind its own goal line and the defense elects to enforce the penalty at that spot. Rule 14, Section 2, Article 2(b) states the same rule from the enforcement side: when the spot of enforcement for a foul by the offense is behind the offensive goal line, it is a safety if the defense elects to enforce the penalty at that spot. Rule 8, Section 7, Article 7(b) applies the same election to a foul during a fumble. Detroit declined the recovery spot and elected the safety. After a safety, Rule 11, Section 5, Article 2 puts the ball in play by a free kick from the scored-upon team's 20-yard line, which is why Chicago kicked off and Detroit got the ball back.

NFL Rulebook — Rule 12, Section 5, Article 1; Rule 11, Section 5, Article 1; Rule 14, Section 2, Article 2
